Model at a glance

What are we trying to model?

Turn a bolete over and the underside of the cap is not gills but a sponge: thousands of tiny tubes, standing side by side, each open at the bottom. What you see is the pattern made by all those openings.

That pattern is what this app draws. It is a flat, two-dimensional picture — the mouths of the tubes — and it changes with age. On a young cap the openings are small and nearly round. As the cap expands they stretch, press against each other, and in some species run together into elongated, maze-like channels.

The question we are asking is modest: what is the simplest set of rules that produces pictures like that? Not every pore drawn by hand — just a few local rules applied everywhere at once.

This is an approximation inspired by mathematical growth systems. It is not a proven account of how a real bolete builds its tubes. Biologists use models of this family, but the actual tissue is more complicated than anything here.

1Seed points

Everything begins with points. Each one marks roughly where the centre of a future pore will be. In the figure below you can drag the points around; one of them is labelled with its position.

Drag a point. The label shows its horizontal and vertical position, measured from the bottom-left corner.

To talk about a point we have to name it and say where it is. Mathematicians write a single point as

Pi = (xi, yi)

Every letter here is doing a plain job:

Pa point — just the name we give to “a seed” ia label saying which point: pore number 1, 2, 3 … The small lowered letter is called a subscript; it is an index, like a numbered tag on a specimen. xhow far to the right the point sits yhow far up the point sits

Two numbers like (x, y) are called coordinates. They are just an address: “go this far across, then this far up, and you will find the point.” A map grid reference works the same way.

2Who owns each piece of space?

Now imagine the cap surface as a field, and the seeds as the places pores will grow from. Each pore claims the ground nearest to it. For every location on the surface we ask one question:

“Which seed is closest to here?”

That location belongs to that seed. Do this everywhere and the surface is carved into regions, one per seed, with every region touching its neighbours. This carving is called a Voronoi tessellation (after the mathematician Georgy Voronoi; “tessellation” just means a tiling with no gaps).

Drag the seeds. Tap anywhere else to drop a probe point: lines are drawn to every seed and the closest one is highlighted — that is the seed the probe location “belongs to”.

To decide which seed is closest we need to measure distance. The rule for the straight-line distance between two points is

d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)

It looks dense, but it is a recipe with four steps:

x₁, y₁the first point’s position; x₂, y₂ the second’s −subtract: how far apart are they horizontally? How far apart vertically? ²multiply each of those gaps by itself (“squared”) +add the two squared gaps together √take the square root — the number which, multiplied by itself, gives the total. This undoes the squaring and returns a plain distance. dthe result: the distance
Drag either point. The horizontal gap and vertical gap form two sides of a right-angled triangle; the distance is the long side. (This is Pythagoras’ theorem wearing coordinates.)

3Why the cells become polygons

Look at the boundary between two neighbouring regions. Every point on it is exactly as far from one seed as from the other — neither pore is closer, so neither wins. The set of all such tie points between two seeds is a straight line, running at right angles across the middle of the line that joins them.

A pore is hemmed in by several neighbours, so its region is cut by several of these straight tie-lines. A shape bounded by straight lines is a polygon. That is the whole reason the cells are polygonal rather than round: each pore pushes outward until it meets the equal-distance line with the next pore, and straight cuts make corners.

Five seeds. Tap a seed to highlight it, then drag any seed: the tie-lines move and the central cell changes shape, but it always stays a polygon.

Notice what we have not done. Nobody drew the cell. We only placed points and applied one rule — “nearest seed wins” — and the polygons appeared on their own.

4The mushroom grows

A cap does not stay the size it was born. It expands outward from its centre, and that expansion is what deforms the pores. To describe growth that spreads out from a centre, (x, y) is a clumsy address. A better one is: how far from the centre, and which direction.

(r, angle)

Mathematicians write the angle with a Greek letter, θ, pronounced “theta”. So a location is (r, θ):

rthe distance from the centre of the cap (“radius”) θthe direction, measured as an angle from a fixed reference line — like a compass bearing
Drag the point. The same location is shown both ways: as (x, y) counted from the corner, and as (r, θ) counted from the centre.

These are called polar coordinates. Nothing about the point has changed; we have only changed how we describe where it is. The reason to bother is the next step: growth that is “outward from the centre” is easy to write in terms of r.

5Different directions can grow differently

Suppose every part of the cap gets a bit bigger. A point that was at distance r from the centre is now farther out. Growth multiplies distance by a factor. We write the factor as G, for “growth”, and put a small letter underneath to say which direction we mean:

Grthe radial growth factor — growth outward, along r Gθthe circumferential growth factor — growth around the cap, along θ
rnew = rold × Gr

In words: the new distance from the centre equals the old distance multiplied by the radial growth amount. A number:

With rold = 10 and Gr = 1.5 the new distance is 10 × 1.5 = 15. The point moved outward. A factor of 1 means no change; below 1 would mean shrinking.

Isotropic and anisotropic

If something grows by the same factor in every direction, we say its growth is isotropic (“iso” = same, “tropic” = turning or direction). A balloon being inflated is close to isotropic: it keeps its shape and just gets bigger.

If it grows more in one direction than another, the growth is anisotropic — “not the same in all directions.” Pulling a piece of chewing gum makes it long in one direction and thin in the other.

For a cap, the comparison that matters is radial against circumferential:

Gr ≈ Gθroughly equal growth in both directions → pores keep their shape, just enlarge Gr > Gθstronger outward growth → pores stretch radially, like spokes Gr < Gθstronger around-the-cap growth → pores stretch sideways, into rings
Isotropic: Gr = Gθ
Anisotropic: Gr ≠ Gθ
The same seeds and the same cells on both sides. Only the growth factors differ. Move the slider past 1 and the right-hand pores lengthen outward; below 1 they flatten into rings.

One subtlety that the simulator reflects. If a cap grows outward much more than around, the stretched tissue would eventually have to tear — or make new pores. Real boletes do add new tubes as they age. The anisotropy slider in the simulator sets how much of that growth difference is expressed as pore shape (high) rather than absorbed, as if by new pores forming (low).

6Biological randomness

A real mushroom is not a perfect mathematical object. Growth differs a little from place to place because of the local tissue, the neighbours pressing in, the stage each pore reached, the physical constraints of the cap, uneven nutrients, and plain microscopic variation.

We can keep all of that out of the equations and still represent it, by saying that the growth at a particular spot is the average growth plus a small wobble:

Glocal = Gbase + ε
Glocalgrowth at this particular location Gbasethe average growth everywhere εa small random, local variation. This is the Greek letter epsilon, which mathematicians habitually use for “a small amount”: an error, a wobble, a noise term.
At ε = 0 the cells are the clean tie-line polygons. Raise ε and two things happen: some pores push harder than their neighbours (cell competition), and the walls pick up small wobbles (boundary noise). The underlying cells never change — only how they are deformed.

In the simulator, ε is split into a few sliders — irregularity of the seeds, competition between neighbours, and boundary noise — so you can see which kind of variation does what. Crucially the random numbers are seeded: the same seed value always produces the same wobbles, which is why a pattern can be reproduced exactly.

Want to go one level deeper? Growth fields and gradients

So far growth has been a single number per direction. A richer idea is that the growth factor can be different at every location — a growth field. We write it

G(x, y)

read “G of x and y”: give me a location, and I will tell you how fast the tissue grows there. (The brackets mean “depends on”, not multiplication.)

Once growth varies from place to place, a natural question is: standing at a point, in which direction does the growth increase fastest, and how steeply? The answer is called the gradient of G, written with an upside-down triangle:

∇G

The triangle is called “nabla” (after an ancient harp of that shape) and is read “grad G”. At every point the gradient is an arrow: it points uphill — toward faster growth — and it is longer where the growth changes more sharply. No calculus is needed to read the picture.

Shading shows the growth field G: brighter means faster growth. Drag the bright patch around. The arrows are ∇G; they always point toward the brightest region and are longest on the slopes, shortest on the flat parts.

Connecting this to the mushroom: if tissue grows faster in one region of the cap than another, pores sitting between the two regions get pulled unevenly, and neighbouring cells deform according to that changing field. The simulator’s radial and circumferential growth factors are a simple special case of a growth field whose value depends only on r.

Want to go two levels deeper? Reaction–diffusion

Scientists who model pattern formation in living tissue — spots, stripes, branching — often use equations that track how an amount of material changes over time as it spreads and reacts. One classic shape of such an equation is

∂H/∂t = D∇²H + growth − loss

Every symbol has a plain meaning:

Hthe amount, or density, of fungal material at a point ttime ∂H/∂thow quickly H is changing as time passes. The curly ∂ is a “partial” d: we are asking only about change over time, holding position fixed. Dthe diffusion strength: how readily the material spreads out to its surroundings ∇²Hthe Laplacian of H. Roughly: how different this spot is from the average of its immediate surroundings. Negative in a hump (higher than around it), positive in a hollow. growth − losswhatever is being added by growth and removed by consumption or decay

So the whole line says: the material at a point changes because it spreads toward (or from) its neighbours, because it grows, and because it is lost. Give the equation two interacting substances and the right constants and it spontaneously produces spots and stripes — the mechanism Alan Turing proposed in 1952.

This app does not need to solve this equation to generate the pore model. The pores you see come from seeds, nearest-point regions and growth factors. The equation is here to show how much deeper mathematical biology can go.

The big idea

The computer was never told what a pore looks like. There is no drawing of a pore anywhere in the program. Instead it was given a short list of local rules and told to apply them everywhere at once:

place pointsdivide the space between neighboursgrow the surfacestretch different directions by different amountsadd small local variations

Complex, lifelike pore patterns emerge from those simple rules. Nobody designed the maze; it is what the rules add up to. This phenomenon — where many simple local interactions produce organised structure at a larger scale — is called emergence, and it shows up throughout nature: in snowflakes, in flocks, in the stripes on a fish.

The simulation demonstrates how complex biological-looking geometry can arise from relatively simple mathematical rules. It should not be read as a complete biological explanation of how real bolete hymenophores develop; what it shows is that such patterns do not need a complicated explanation to exist.
